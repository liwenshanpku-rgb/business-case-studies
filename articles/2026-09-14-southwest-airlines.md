# Southwest Airlines

**The setup**: Through the 1960s, U.S. commercial aviation was a tightly regulated club. The Civil Aeronautics Board (CAB) controlled which airlines could fly which interstate routes and, crucially, set the fares — no carrier could compete on price. The big trunk carriers (American, Braniff, Continental, Delta) competed instead on schedules, meals, and marketing, all while enjoying fares set high enough to protect everyone's margins. Fixed costs were also inflated by the "full-service" model: hub-and-spoke networks, mixed fleets, assigned seating, meal service, and interline baggage transfers with other carriers. Nobody could break ranks on price even if they wanted to, because the regulator wouldn't let them.

**What they actually did differently**: Herb Kelleher and Rollin King incorporated Air Southwest in 1967 and launched flights in 1971 under a narrow but powerful loophole: if an airline flew only *within* a single state, it fell outside CAB jurisdiction entirely. Southwest flew only between Dallas, Houston, and San Antonio — all inside Texas — which let it set its own fares free of CAB price floors. Three incumbents (Braniff, Trans-Texas, and Continental) sued to block Southwest before it ever got off the ground, but the Texas Supreme Court threw out the injunction in an emergency session. Once flying, a 1972 cash crunch forced Southwest to sell one of its four Boeing 737s to raise $500,000 — leaving it needing to run four planes' worth of schedule with three aircraft. VP of operations Bill Franklin's answer was the "10-minute turn": clean, refuel, and reboard a plane at the gate in a tenth of the industry-standard 45 minutes. That single operational constraint cascaded into the whole business model — one aircraft type only (the 737, so pilots, mechanics, and parts were interchangeable), no meals to load, no seat assignments to manage, no interline baggage agreements to honor, and point-to-point routing instead of hub-and-spoke. Every piece reinforced the others.

**Why incumbents couldn't just copy it**: The regulatory shield was the first barrier — CAB-regulated carriers legally could not just start undercutting fares on their interstate networks even if they wanted to. But the deeper reason Southwest stayed uncopyable for decades even after deregulation in 1978 was organizational and structural, not legal. The major carriers had already built their entire cost base around hub-and-spoke connectivity, mixed fleets accumulated through decades of acquisitions, unionized labor agreements tied to those complex operations, and revenue systems (interline ticketing, connecting itineraries, loyalty programs feeding a hub) that depended on the very complexity Southwest had stripped out. Copying the 10-minute turn in isolation was easy to describe and nearly impossible to do — it required simultaneously giving up assigned seating, meal service, connecting traffic revenue, and fleet diversity, each of which some internal constituency (labor, marketing, a hub-city government, a fleet-leasing contract) had a vested interest in keeping. Southwest wasn't protected by a patent or a technology; it was protected by the fact that unwinding an incumbent's accumulated commitments was organizationally harder than building a low-cost operation from a blank sheet of paper.

**What eventually happened**: The formula worked spectacularly for a long time. Southwest turned a profit every year from 1973 through 2019 — an unmatched streak in an industry famous for bankruptcies — and low-cost carriers worldwide (Ryanair, easyJet, JetBlue) explicitly modeled themselves on it after deregulation opened U.S. skies in 1978. But the ending isn't clean. COVID broke the profit streak, a catastrophic scheduling-system meltdown stranded passengers over the 2022 holidays, and by 2024 activist investor Elliott Management had taken a stake and forced boardroom and strategy changes. Since then, Southwest has dismantled the very features that once defined it: it ended 53 years of open seating in January 2026, moving to assigned seats like every rival it used to undercut, and killed its "bags fly free" policy in May 2025. The model that broke the industry's mold eventually converged back toward the industry norm once its cost advantages narrowed and its differentiation stopped being what investors wanted.

**The generalizable pattern**: Southwest's edge came from exploiting a regulatory boundary and then building an internally consistent low-cost operating system around it — not from better jets or better technology (it flew the same 737 everyone else could buy). Incumbents couldn't respond because matching any one piece meant unwinding years of labor contracts, network commitments, and revenue dependencies — the moat was organizational inertia, not a legal patent. And even a business-model innovation isn't permanent: once the regulatory gap closed, imitators multiplied, and shifting customer expectations and investor pressure eventually pulled Southwest itself back toward the industry-standard model it once disrupted.
